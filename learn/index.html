How to read what AlgoReach shows you.

Every figure in the terminal is shown with its source, its time and its uncertainty. These briefs explain why, and how to read a score, a fit or a data panel without over-reading it.

Why we share scores, fits and data as is

A research tool that hides its working asks to be trusted. AlgoReach asks to be checked instead. Scores carry their evidence and counter-case, fits carry their coverage and out-of-sample result, and data panels carry the source, licence and timestamp of every value. If a reader can see where a number came from, they can disagree with it intelligently, and that is the point.

  • A number without its source is an opinion. Every value names where it came from and when.
  • A probability without its calibration history is a guess. Every probability says whether it has been calibrated.
  • A record without its misses is marketing. The ledger keeps every call, scored, including the ones that were wrong.

Example

Two tools both say a stock is likely to rise. One shows only the arrow. The other shows that the call came from an order-win filing at 11:05, that calls of this kind came true 6 times in 10 in the past, and that a fall in the index below its last low would make the call wrong. Only the second can be checked, and argued with.

Why AlgoReach does this

Markets punish confident numbers that cannot be traced. Showing the working lets a reader find the weak link before money depends on it, and it keeps the engine honest: a figure that has to be shown has to be defensible.

Reading a score

A score is a calibrated probability that a stated outcome happens within a stated horizon, for a stated event type. Three things to read with it: the calibration bucket (how often similar stated probabilities came true in the past), the evidence list (which measured links supported it) and the counter-case (what would make it wrong).

01

Calibrated

The stated probability has been compared with outcomes for this kind of call. Read it at face value, with the bucket's own error.

02

Uncalibrated

Too little history yet. Treat it as a direction with a reason, not a probability.

03

Horizon

The number of trading days the call is about. A score is not a view beyond its horizon.

04

Counter-case

The condition under which the engine would score itself wrong. If it is already true, the score is stale.

What AlgoReach does not publish: the detector rules, weights and thresholds behind a score. What it does publish: how often the scores were right, by type and bucket.

Example

A score reads 65% that the stock closes higher in 5 sessions after a credit-rating upgrade, marked Calibrated. It means: of past calls stated near 65% for upgrades at a 5-session horizon, roughly 65 in 100 came true, within that bucket's error. It does not mean the price will rise, and it says nothing about session 6.

Why AlgoReach does this

A bare up or down arrow hides how sure the engine is. A probability tied to its own track record lets you size how much weight to give it, and lets the ledger prove later whether the 65% deserved to be 65%.

Reading a fit

A fit is a measured relationship between a crowd-behaviour series and what the market did afterwards, for example between a divergence of stated and revealed sentiment and the price path that followed. A fit is only shown after it was pre-registered as a hypothesis and checked on data it had not seen.

  • Coverage first: a fit over a thin series is visibly thin, and the page says so.
  • Pre-registration date: the question was written down before the answer was measured.
  • Out-of-sample: the result on data the fit was not tuned on is the one that counts.
  • A failed fit stays on the failed-ideas list and is not re-tuned until it passes.

Example

Suppose the hypothesis, written down first, is: when a name's news mood turns sharply positive while institutions are net sellers that week, the next 5 sessions lag the index. It is then tested only on weeks it never saw. If the measured effect sits inside what random dates give, the fit is marked NULL and stays off.

Why AlgoReach does this

With enough tries, any pattern looks real on old data. Writing the question first and judging it on unseen data is how AlgoReach avoids selling a coincidence as a discovery; a NULL is published because it saves you from it too.

Reading data as is

Prices, flows, filings and positioning are shown as they were recorded: with a UTC timestamp, a source and a licence tag, and a data-age badge when a panel is older than its usual refresh. Nothing is smoothed, back-filled or re-stated without a visible note.

01

Live

Read during the session from the live feed; the panel says so and shows the last tick time.

02

Last fetched

The most recent stored value with its fetch time. Older than its cadence means the source was slow or blocked.

03

Session close

The last close, including Friday's close over a weekend. Never presented as live.

04

Blocked source

A source that refused automated access is not fetched, and the gap is shown rather than filled.

Example

On a Saturday the price panel shows Friday's close with the tag Session close and the time it was fetched, not a live-looking number. If the shareholding source refused automated access this week, the panel shows the gap and the date of the last good value instead of carrying an old figure forward.

Why AlgoReach does this

A value that looks live but is a day old leads to a wrong decision at the worst moment. Showing source, time and freshness on every panel means you always know what you are looking at.

Shadow periods and planned items

New analytics ship switched off, run in shadow for at least five trading days, and are compared with live before anyone sees them. A tile marked Planned on this site describes work that is not built yet; a tile marked Built names a page that exists in the terminal today.

Example

A new alert rule is switched on in shadow: it writes what it would have sent for at least five trading days, nobody receives it, and the shadow log is compared with what actually happened before it goes live.

Why AlgoReach does this

A rule that misfires on day one costs trust that takes months to rebuild. Shadow first means a new idea is judged on real sessions before it can reach you.

What the model is learning

Calibration improves as calls reach their horizon and are scored; coverage improves as sources stay green and series lengthen; fits are added only when they survive pre-registration and an out-of-sample check. The terminal's coming-soon page shows those counters as they stand.

None of this is advice. It is a way of reading research output so that it informs a decision you make yourself.

Example

A new event type starts Uncalibrated. After enough of its calls have reached their horizon and been scored, its probabilities can be checked against outcomes, and the label changes only when the record supports it.

Why AlgoReach does this

Learning that is not measured is just drift. Counting scored calls, healthy sources and surviving fits shows how much the engine actually knows today, not how much it hopes to.
